PulseHawk Upgrade Report, specification v1.0

Version 1.0 of the Upgrade Report specification, as published on 2 November 2026. It stays online unchanged so that every report that follows it can be checked against it.

What does an Upgrade Report measure?

An Upgrade Report covers one network upgrade on one PoW or DAG chain. Its window runs from 24 hours before to 72 hours after the first block at the activation height. All times are in UTC. Block times are the timestamps in the block headers that our own node or header client receives.

The target interval and the activation height each come from two independent sources, a protocol constant and a release. If the two differ, no report is built.

Which values does every Upgrade Report carry?

  • M1 Block intervals. p50, p90 and p99 of the block intervals, before and after the activation height, each against the value computed for an ideal chain at the target interval.
  • M2 Longest gap. The longest block interval and the number of intervals above five times the target.
  • M3 Reorgs. The number of reorgs and their maximum depth in blocks.
  • M4 Chain split. Heights with competing tips, the most tips at one height and how long the longest lasted.
  • M5 Peers on pre-upgrade software. The share of peers in our sample that run pre-upgrade software at activation, 24 hours later and 72 hours later, with n.
  • M6 Time to stable. The time from the activation block until the rolling 1 hour p50 of block intervals first lies within 10 % of the p50 computed for an ideal chain at the new target.
  • M7 First block at the activation height. Its time and the gap to our own estimate published before activation.

How are the values computed?

  • Two sets of intervals. The interval of a block is its time minus the time of the block before it. Blocks below the activation height form the set before activation, blocks above it the set after. The interval that ends at the activation block belongs to neither set.
  • Percentiles. A percentile is the value at rank ceil(q × n) of the sorted intervals, without interpolation.
  • Ideal chain. For a chain with exponential intervals at target T, p50 is T × ln 2, p90 is T × ln 10 and p99 is T × ln 100, rounded to 0.1 s. These values are computed.
  • Gaps. M2 counts intervals strictly above five times the target.
  • Reorgs and tips. A reorg is a switch of our node to a competing branch, its depth the number of blocks replaced. A tip competes when another block at the same height reaches our node, and lasts until one of them is abandoned.

How are peers and time to stable counted?

  • Peer samples. Our node asks the peers it connects to for their software version. A peer counts as pre-upgrade when its version cannot follow the upgrade. There are three samples, at activation, after 24 hours and after 72 hours, each within one hour of its time. Only the version is kept, no address.
  • Time to stable. For every block from one hour after activation on, we take the p50 of the intervals in the hour before it. Time to stable is the time from the activation block to the first block where that p50 lies within 10 % of T × ln 2 at the new target. If no block does within the window, the report says so.
  • Our estimate. M7 compares the time of the activation block with our own estimate, published before activation with its date.

What does the data file contain?

Each report publishes one CSV with the columns report_id, method_version, kind, label, height, time_utc and value. A row of kind block holds a height, its header time and the interval to the block before. Rows of kind reorg hold the depth, rows of kind tip the duration in seconds, and rows of kind peer the sample label and 1 for pre-upgrade software or 0 for upgraded software.

The page shows the sha256 of the CSV. Every value on the page is computed again from that file before the page is built.
