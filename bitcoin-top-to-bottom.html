Why other sites show different dates and prices

You will find the last top quoted as 126,198 on 6 October 2025. You will find the 2021 top on 10 November and the 2022 low on 21 November. None of those are wrong, and neither are ours. They are intraday extremes from a single exchange. Ours are daily closes.

We use closes for one reason: they can be checked. An intraday print exists on one venue for one second. A daily close is the same number for everyone who looks at the same day. It moves the counts by a day or two either side and moves the top by about 1,400 dollars, and it changes nothing about the shape: all three completed cycles still land between 364 and 406 days.

The 2013 top is the one that moves most between methods. Intraday sources put it on 30 November 2013. The highest daily close came five days later, on 5 December. We use the close, and we say so.

What this does not tell you

It does not tell you when this cycle bottoms. Three cycles are three data points, they differ by 42 days, and the market they traded in is not the market of 2015. Search this question and most results will name you a month. We will not, and that is deliberate.

It also does not tell you a price. We publish no price targets, in any format, ever. The bottom prices in the table are history, not a template.

How we count

Every price on this page is a daily close in UTC from Twelve Data, held in PulseHawk's own archive of 5,849 daily closes from 18 August 2010 to today. A cycle top is the highest close before the next cycle's top; a cycle bottom is the lowest close between two tops. Days are calendar days between those two closes, with the top day as day zero. The comparison table looks up each past cycle on the same day number and divides that day's close by that cycle's top.

Archive built once and appended daily. Nothing on this page is sourced from CoinGecko, CoinMarketCap, Bitcoin Magazine or any other site's tables.
